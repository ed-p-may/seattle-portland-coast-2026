Seattle

The Library, Rainier Tower, and Freeway Park sit within four blocks of each other; Pioneer Square is a short drive or a ten-minute walk downhill. Park once.

Seattle Central Library

OMA (Rem Koolhaas, Joshua Prince-Ramus) with LMN Architects · structure: Arup / Magnusson Klemencic

The programme was sorted into five fixed blocks — parking, staff, meeting, books, administration — stacked and shifted so each one gets the light and access it needs, and the leftover space between them became the public rooms. A single diagonal steel diagrid skin, glazed and screened, wraps the whole shifted stack, which is why the building looks unstable and is not.

The Books Spiral is the part worth the trip: the entire nonfiction collection on one continuous 2%-grade ramp running through four floors, so the Dewey sequence never breaks at a stair landing. Below it, the Mixing Chamber is the reference floor; above, the Level 10 reading room sits under the folded glass.

Do it top-down. Take the escalator or lift to Level 10, then walk the Spiral to Level 6. The red corridor on Level 4 and the chartreuse escalators are not decoration — they are wayfinding for a building with no repeating floor plate.

Terrain: step-free from Fourth Avenue, lifts to every level, and the Spiral itself is a gentle ramp with seating. The easiest major building on this list.

The Spheres · “the Amazon balls”

NBBJ, 2018 · structure: Magnusson Klemencic · horticulture: Ben Eiben

Three intersecting glass domes, 80–90 ft across, framed on the geometry of a pentagonal hexecontahedron — sixty pentagonal faces rather than the usual geodesic triangulation, which is what gives the steel skin its unusually open, non-triangular read. Roughly 2,600 panes of glass carrying a low-e coating tuned for plants instead of people: the envelope has to hold a 72 °F / 60% RH cloud-forest climate against Seattle winter, and it is conditioned partly off waste heat from a neighbouring data centre.

The catch: the plant-filled interior is only open to the public on the first and third Saturday of the month, by free reservation released fifteen days ahead. Your Seattle days are Monday and Tuesday, so the interior is not available this trip.

What is available is Understory, the free exhibit space underneath, open Monday–Saturday 10 a.m.–8 p.m. with no ticket — it gets you under the glass, explains the structure and the horticulture, and takes twenty minutes. Walk the perimeter at street level afterwards; the geometry reads better from the Lenora Street side.

Terrain: flat sidewalk, step-free entry to Understory, benches nearby. Street parking downtown is difficult — a garage on Sixth or Seventh is the realistic option.

Olympic Sculpture Park

Weiss/Manfredi · landscape: Charles Anderson · SAM, nine acres

The site was a Unocal fuel-transfer brownfield cut twice — by Elliott Avenue and by an active BNSF main line — and dropping forty feet from the city to the water. Rather than bridge the cuts, Weiss/Manfredi built a continuous 2,200-foot Z-shaped landform that crosses both on its way down, so the road and the railway pass underneath a park you never have to step off.

The descent runs through four planted zones in sequence: evergreen forest, deciduous grove, meadow, and shore. Serra’s Wake, Calder’s Eagle, and Oldenburg’s Typewriter Eraser are placed as landmarks along the fold. The PACCAR Pavilion at the top is the only enclosed building.

Free, never ticketed, and open every day — the most reliable outdoor option if Monday afternoon runs loose.

Terrain: the Z-path is an accessible continuous grade with no stairs, but it is a long, sloped 2,200 ft down and back. Benches throughout. Driving to the lower Alaskan Way end and walking partway up is the short-segment version.

Rainier Tower and Rainier Square Tower

Minoru Yamasaki, 1977 · NBBJ with Magnusson Klemencic, 2020

Yamasaki set a 41-storey tower — some thirty floors of it above an eleven-storey reinforced-concrete pedestal that tapers downward to a narrow base, freeing the ground plane for a plaza and shops. Locals call it the beaver-chewed stump. It is a deliberate inversion of the usual podium-and-tower logic, and Yamasaki built it four years after finishing the World Trade Center.

Next door, NBBJ’s 850-foot Rainier Square Tower (2020) carves its own base away on the diagonal specifically so the pedestal stays visible, and it is structurally the more significant of the pair: it was the first high-rise anywhere built with SpeedCore, a concrete-filled composite steel-plate shear-wall core that replaces rebar and formwork entirely. The core went up four floors a week and took ten months off a thirty-two-month schedule.

Five minutes of looking, on the way between the Library and Freeway Park. Stand at Fifth and University to see both bases at once.

Terrain: flat sidewalk, no entry required. The through-block plaza has seating.

Pioneer Building

Elmer H. Fisher for Henry Yesler · National Historic Landmark, 1977

Six storeys of rusticated stone, red brick, terra cotta, and cast iron at the corner of First and James, and the single best specimen of what the 1889 fire produced. The fire took thirty-odd blocks of wooden Seattle; the city responded with an ordinance requiring masonry, and Fisher — who designed somewhere near fifty buildings in the eighteen months that followed — became the author of the district’s Richardsonian Romanesque character almost by volume alone.

The excavation predates the fire; the building was finished in 1892, the year Yesler died. Look for the deep arched entry, the heavy stone base carrying progressively lighter brick above, and the ornate light well in the lobby. During the 1896 Klondike rush as many as forty-eight mining companies kept offices inside it.

The block itself matters as much as the building: Pioneer Square was Washington’s first historic district (1970), and the cast-iron pergola, the Yesler and Mutual Life buildings, and the regraded street level are all part of the same post-fire argument.

Terrain: flat, paved, benches in the square. Skip the Underground Tour — stairs, standing, and crowding.

Freeway Park

Lawrence Halprin & Associates · designed by Angela Danadjieva

The first park in the world built as a lid over an urban freeway — five acres of board-formed concrete canyon decked across I-5, stitching First Hill back to downtown after the freeway severed them. Danadjieva, the same designer behind Portland’s Keller Fountain, built the Canyon fountain out of stacked concrete boxes that swallow the traffic noise underneath.

Worth seeing directly against Keller on Wednesday: same office, same designer, six years apart, one as a civic forecourt and one as infrastructure repair. It is also a lesson in how this kind of landscape ages — sight lines, maintenance, and the long rehabilitation effort are all legible.

Terrain: multi-level with stairs between terraces, though the Seneca Street and Convention Center entries are level. Ten minutes is enough.

Bullitt Center

The Miller Hull Partnership, 2013 · first Living Building Challenge–certified commercial office

Six storeys of FSC-certified heavy timber on a concrete base, net-positive energy on a 14,000 sq ft rooftop PV array that oversails the building like a hat, rainwater-to-potable treatment, composting toilets, geothermal wells, and no parking. The envelope is the interesting part for anyone who does this for a living: Schüco aluminium-framed triple-glazed units reaching whole-assembly U-values around 0.17, automated exterior blinds, motor-operated night-flush ventilation, and a daylight-autonomy target that drove the 14-foot floor-to-floor heights, the window heads run to the ceiling, and the narrow floor plate.

The famous “irresistible stair” is glazed and hung on the street corner on purpose — the lift is deliberately made the duller choice. It has held net-positive since opening.

Exterior and ground-floor lobby are freely visible. Group tours are arranged in advance and are the only way to see the mechanical rooms.

Terrain: flat corner site on Capitol Hill, five minutes by car from downtown, easy street parking compared with the core.

Chapel of St. Ignatius

Steven Holl Architects · Seattle University

Holl’s watercolour concept was “seven bottles of light in a stone box,” and the building is exactly that: a tilt-up concrete box, poured on site in twenty-one panels and raised in a single day, punctured by seven differently shaped roof scoops. Each scoop pairs a coloured baffle with a complementary lens, so what lands in the room is coloured light bounced off a surface you never see directly.

The seven volumes correspond to parts of Jesuit worship. The reflecting pond, the cast-bronze door, and the hand-troweled plaster are all part of a very small building that repays fifteen minutes.

Terrain: level campus paths, step-free entry, seating inside. Ten minutes from Capitol Hill; pairs naturally with the Bullitt Center.

Portland

1970Free · park open 5 a.m.–9 p.m.

Ira Keller Fountain

Lawrence Halprin & Associates · designed by Angela Danadjieva

A full city block of board-formed concrete terraces stepping down to an 18-foot drop carrying 13,000 gallons a minute. Danadjieva abstracted the Columbia Gorge waterfalls — not as imagery, but as the actual behaviour of water breaking over stacked basalt ledges — and Halprin sited it as a forecourt to the auditorium opposite, so the fountain is the room’s stage rather than its ornament.

The point of it is that you are meant to go in. There are no fences, no keep-off signage, and the platforms above the falls are reachable on foot. It won an ASLA medallion in 1999 and it is the reason Halprin’s reputation went international in 1970.

It is the third and best of the Portland Open Space Sequence — Lovejoy Fountain (1966), Pettygrove Park (1968), Keller (1970) — four blocks of connected landscape built as one argument about how a city gets water, grade, and public room. If you have twenty extra minutes, walk the sequence in order.

Terrain: the upper plaza on SW 3rd is flat and reaches the best viewing point with no steps. The terraces themselves are uneven concrete with no handrails — look from above. Water typically runs until early November; if it matters, call Portland Parks at 503-823-7529 before going.

The Portland Building

Michael Graves, 1982 · reconstruction: DLR Group, 2019 · NRHP-listed

The first major public building of American Postmodernism, won in competition by a then-obscure Graves and built for about $51 a square foot. A square, squat block in cream and terracotta with a garlanded keystone, oversized pilasters, and a small ribbon of tinted windows — the entire argument being that a civic building could be figurative and cheap at the same time. It was disliked from the day it opened and listed on the National Register anyway, at only twenty-nine years old, as a building of exceptional importance.

The envelope failed almost immediately. The painted-concrete walls leaked from roughly 1987 onward and thirty years of patching did not stop it. DLR Group’s $195m reconstruction, finished in 2019, left the original concrete in place as a substrate and overclad the whole building: a unitized painted-metal rainscreen curtain wall on floors 4–15 with larger clear double-glazed windows replacing Graves’s unpopular black tint, and mechanically hung terra cotta as a rainscreen over the three-storey base.

Worth arguing about on the sidewalk. Preservation orthodoxy says restore; this project replicated the appearance in new materials to solve a water problem the original detailing could never solve, and got a fifty-to-hundred-year building out of it. Portlandia, Raymond Kaskey’s hammered-copper figure, is still above the Fifth Avenue entrance and is the second-largest such statue in the United States after the Statue of Liberty.

Terrain: flat, and it faces Chapman Square. Keller Fountain is three blocks south; the Commonwealth Building is a third of a mile north.

Pioneer Courthouse Square

Willard K. Martin with Will Martin Architects · “Portland’s living room”

Not to be confused with Seattle’s Pioneer Square neighbourhood — this is a single downtown block, and the most successful piece of civic place-making in the city. The site held the Portland Hotel until 1951, then spent thirty years as a surface car park before a 1980 competition and a public campaign turned it back into a square.

The plan is a brick amphitheatre stepping down to a stage, framed by a classical portico and the surviving wrought-iron gate of the old hotel. Roughly 65,000 of the bricks carry the names of the people who bought them for $15 each to fund construction — the building method and the funding mechanism are the same gesture, which is why the square has held its public affection for forty years. MAX light rail runs along two sides, so it is also the transit hub.

Across Sixth is Pioneer Courthouse itself (Alfred B. Mullett, 1875), the oldest federal building in the Pacific Northwest, restored in 2005 with a seismic base-isolation retrofit.

Terrain: the amphitheatre is stepped, but the perimeter sidewalk and the SW Yamhill edge are level and give you the whole space. Public restrooms on site.

Union Way

LEVER Architecture (Thomas Robinson), 2013

A daylit covered arcade cut straight through two early-twentieth-century garage buildings to connect Burnside at Powell’s to Stark at the Ace Hotel — 200 feet of new public right-of-way created by subtraction rather than construction. The reference is the Parisian passage and the Tokyo shopping lane, rewritten for a wet climate: a continuous skylit vault, white glazed brick, and small shopfronts at a scale downtown Portland had stopped building.

It cost almost nothing relative to its effect on the block, and it is the project that made LEVER’s reputation before their mass-timber work. Worth noticing what it does to the two cross streets, and how the section handles rain and light without mechanical ventilation.

This is free, takes four minutes, and sits precisely between two stops already on Wednesday’s plan — Powell’s and Jake’s Famous Crawfish are a three-minute walk apart and Union Way is the connector.

Terrain: level, covered, sheltered end to end. The easiest stop on the Portland list.

Tanner Springs Park

Atelier Dreiseitl (Herbert Dreiseitl) with GreenWorks PC

One Pearl District block returned, as literally as a city block can be, to the wetland it was. Tanner Creek still runs under these streets in a pipe; the park pulls groundwater up into a spring at the high end, runs it through a planted terrace of native wetland, and holds it in a pond at the low corner. The whole thing is a closed loop on a remediated brownfield — the water you see is cleaned and recirculated, not municipal.

The Artwall is the piece people remember: 368 salvaged railroad rails stood on end in a 200-foot wave, with 99 hand-fused glass inserts showing dragonflies, amphibians, and insects. The rails are a direct reference to the rail yard that occupied the site. Weathering steel, no coating, intentionally rusting.

Read it as the third act of a deliberate Pearl sequence: Jamison Square (Peter Walker, 2002) is the urban plaza with the stepped fountain, Tanner Springs is the wild one, and The Fields (2013) is the open lawn. Three blocks apart on NW 10th/11th.

Terrain: the perimeter path is level and paved; the boardwalk descends gently. Benches. Street parking in the Pearl is metered but usually findable.

Peninsula Park & Rose Garden

Emanuel T. Mische, 1912–13 · bandstand: Ellis F. Lawrence

Portland’s first public rose garden and still the only sunken one in Oregon: a formal French parterre knot garden set below grade, symmetrical, geometrically laid out around a central cast fountain, with roughly 8,900 plantings in about 65 varieties across two acres. Mische trained with the Olmsted Brothers and ran Portland’s parks from 1908 to 1914, which is why this reads as a City Beautiful set piece rather than a municipal flower bed. It drew 300,000 visitors in its first year.

The bandstand at the head of the garden is by Ellis F. Lawrence — the architect who founded the University of Oregon’s school of architecture — and it is the last surviving bandstand of its kind in the city, a designated Portland Historic Landmark in its own right.

Late September is past peak bloom but the garden still carries colour, and the structure is the point here anyway: the sunken plane, the axis, the hedge walls, the fountain, the bandstand terminating the view. Twenty minutes.

Terrain: short, flat, paved, free, with seating and an accessible restroom. Already in Wednesday’s plan at 5:10–5:50 p.m.; Eem, the Wednesday dinner candidate, is a short drive south.

700 N Rosa Parks Way · North Portland

Portland Japanese Garden

Takuma Tono, 1963–67 · Cultural Village: Kengo Kuma & Associates, 2017

Twelve acres on the site of the old Washington Park zoo, laid out by Takuma Tono of Tokyo Agricultural University as eight distinct garden types — Flat Garden, Strolling Pond, Tea Garden, Natural Garden, Sand and Stone, and so on — each a different formal proposition rather than one continuous landscape. It is routinely called the most authentic Japanese garden outside Japan.

The 2017 Cultural Village is Kengo Kuma’s first public commission in the United States and the reason this belongs on an architecture list rather than a garden one: three pavilions arranged around a courtyard, castle-style dry-stacked stone walls built by Japanese castle-wall masons, and Kuma’s signature deep layered timber eaves with living green roofs. The Umami Café sits on the terrace.

Buy timed tickets online before going; it is busy even midweek.

Terrain: honest warning — this one is steep. The entry is a long uphill approach (a free shuttle runs from the ticket gate to the top), and once inside, several gardens are on sloped gravel and stone paths with steps and no rail. The Flat Garden, Cultural Village, and café terrace are the reachable, level part; the Natural Garden and Strolling Pond are not. Worth it for the Kuma buildings alone if the full garden is too much.

Commonwealth Building (Equitable Building)

Pietro Belluschi, 1948 · NRHP 1976 · ASME Mechanical Engineering Landmark 1980

The most historically important building on this entire list, and almost nobody stops at it. Belluschi finished the first fully glass-and-aluminium-sheathed modernist office tower in the United States here in 1948 — before Lever House (1952) and before Mies’s Lake Shore Drive apartments. A high-strength concrete frame slimmed down to the minimum, sheathed flush in aluminium panels and green-tinted glass, with no expressed structure and no punched openings.

The part that matters to anyone who works on envelopes: the glass is hermetically sealed double-pane thermal units, in 1948, and the reason Belluschi could get away with that much glazing at all is that the building was conditioned by a reverse-cycle heat pump drawing on groundwater — the first large-scale application of that technology anywhere. Sealed IGUs and a groundwater-source heat pump, two years after the war. ASME made the mechanical system a National Historic Mechanical Engineering Landmark in 1980.

About a third of a mile north of the Portland Building, two blocks from Pioneer Courthouse Square. Five minutes on the sidewalk, and it will reframe every glass box you have ever complained about.

Terrain: flat, on the downtown loop, no entry required.

Oregon Coast

The coast is engineering and vernacular rather than signature architecture: Depression-era bridges, a wartime timber hangar, a Victorian captain’s house, and a monument. Most of it is on the days already planned.

Museum closed · storm damageExterior visible from US-101

Hangar B · former Naval Air Station Tillamook

U.S. Navy Bureau of Yards and Docks, 1942–43

Believed to be the largest clear-span wooden structure in the world: 1,072 ft long, 296 ft wide, 192 ft to the crown — fifteen storeys — carried on fifty-one timber catenary arch trusses. It is made of wood only because steel was rationed; the Navy needed somewhere to keep eight K-class blimps that flew anti-submarine patrol over the shipping lanes from California to the San Juans, and wood was what was available. Its twin, Hangar A, was built in twenty-seven days and burned in 1992.

Anyone who has framed a building should see it. The catenary is doing exactly what a catenary does, at a scale that makes the logic unmistakable from the highway.

Terrain and status: the air museum has been closed until further notice since a December 2025 windstorm tore through the south roof, and it has not reopened. The hangar itself is still standing and is plainly visible from US-101 south of Tillamook — treat this as a slow drive-past, not a stop, and check the site before assuming otherwise.

Astoria Column

Electus D. Litchfield · frieze: Attilio Pusterla · funded by Vincent Astor and Ralph Budd

A 125-foot reinforced-concrete column on Coxcomb Hill, modelled on Trajan’s Column in Rome and the Vendôme column in Paris, and wrapped in a 525-foot spiral frieze that climbs fourteen times around it. Pusterla rendered the frieze in sgraffito — successive layers of tinted plaster cut back to expose the colour beneath — which had never been used on a monumental column anywhere before this one.

It was paid for by the Great Northern Railway and John Jacob Astor’s great-grandson to mark the railway reaching Astoria, which is the real subject of the top of the frieze. It was dedicated in 1926 and the city is marking its centennial this year.

The base is free to walk to and the view over the Columbia bar, the bridge, and the Coast Range is the whole point. Balsa gliders are sold at the gift shop and throwing one off the top is a local institution.

Terrain: the parking lot, the lawn, and the base of the column are level, with the full view available without climbing. The observation deck is 164 steps up a narrow interior spiral with no lift — skip it and lose very little.

Astoria–Megler Bridge

William Adair Bugge, Oregon and Washington highway departments · 4.1 miles

The longest continuous truss bridge in North America, crossing the mouth of the Columbia where the river is four miles wide and the bar is one of the most dangerous in the world. The 1,232-foot cantilever-truss main span sits 200 feet above the navigation channel at the Astoria end — high enough for ocean shipping — and then the deck drops into a long low causeway of plate-girder spans across the shallows before rising again in Washington.

That profile is the design: the engineering problem was not the span, it was building four miles of structure through a tidal estuary in a place where wind and current make everything harder. It ended the last ferry on US-101 and completed the highway from Mexico to Canada.

Best seen from the Astoria Riverwalk or from the Column, where the whole four miles reads at once.

Terrain: nothing to walk. Viewpoints along the Riverwalk near the Maritime Museum are flat, paved, and have parking.

Captain George Flavel House Museum

Carl W. Leick · Queen Anne · National Register listed

Built for the first Columbia River bar pilot to make a fortune off it, and the best-preserved Queen Anne house on the Oregon coast. Leick — who designed lighthouses and life-saving stations up and down this coast — gave it an asymmetrical massing, a four-storey octagonal tower positioned so Flavel could watch his own ships cross the bar, and six fireplaces with imported hardwood mantels and tile surrounds.

It is a carpentry building as much as an architectural one: the original millwork, the stair, and the interior finishes are largely intact, which is unusual for the type.

Terrain: a historic house with a stepped porch and a staircase to the upper floors; the ground floor and grounds are the accessible part. Fifteen minutes from the Maritime Museum, already on Sunday.

Columbia River Maritime Museum

1982 waterfront building · Barbey Center and Mariners Hall: Opsis Architecture

The 1982 building is the reason it reads from the water: a glass-and-steel hall under a sweeping wave-form roof, 37,000 sq ft on a 2.5-acre river site, built as a deliberately legible piece of civic shorthand for the Columbia bar. The later Opsis-designed halls are the more disciplined architecture — long-span timber and glass framing views back to the river and the bridge.

The lightship Columbia is moored outside and is itself worth the look. Already the Sunday morning anchor in the itinerary, with free accessible parking and loaner wheelchairs.

Terrain: the main museum is level and accessible with drop-off parking; some vessel and platform areas are not.

Tillamook Creamery

Olson Kundig (Tom Kundig, with Alan Maskin on exhibits), 2018

A 42,800 sq ft visitor building that is more interesting than a cheese stop has any right to be: a modern barn in plan and section, with hand-waxed structural steel, exposed columns, polished concrete floors, and a 25,000 sq ft cross-laminated timber roof — one of the larger early CLT roof decks in the United States and a good look at mass timber doing its actual job rather than appearing in a rendering.

Kundig’s argument was to build something rational and agricultural rather than themed, which is why the structure is left visible. The viewing gallery over the packaging line is genuinely good industrial theatre.

Terrain: flat, step-free, lifts, large car park. Go off-peak — it is one of Oregon’s busiest attractions. See the food guide for the eating strategy.

The US-101 corridor: McCullough bridges and WPA stonework

Conde B. McCullough, Oregon State Highway Department · CCC/WPA masonry, 1930s

The Oregon Coast Highway is the most sustained piece of Depression-era infrastructure design in the country, and McCullough is the reason. As state bridge engineer he built more than 600 bridges and insisted, against the economics, that each one be composed — reinforced-concrete arches with Gothic and Art Deco detailing, obelisk pylons, and balustrades, sited so the structure frames the crossing instead of interrupting it.

On your route: the Wilson River Bridge on US-101 at Tillamook (1931) is the McCullough you will actually drive over. Further south are his masterpieces at Yaquina Bay, Siuslaw, and Coos Bay, if a future trip ever goes that way.

Separately, watch the guardrails and retaining walls where US-101 climbs Neahkahnie Mountain just north of Manzanita: that hand-laid basalt is 1930s relief-era stonework, built by crews with hand tools, and it is still the edge of the road. The viewpoint pullouts on that climb are part of the same programme.

Terrain: all of it is visible from the car. The Neahkahnie pullouts have level parking and a guardrail view with no walking required.

How to actually use this

Field notes

Six things that will decide whether any of this happens.

SEA / 01

Park once downtown

Library, Rainier Tower, Freeway Park, and Pioneer Square are one garage apart. Street parking in the Seattle core is the slow way to lose an afternoon. The Spheres are a separate stop in Denny Triangle.

SEA / 02

The Spheres interior is out

Public interior access is first and third Saturdays only, reserved fifteen days ahead. Monday and Tuesday get you Understory, which is free, walk-in, and open until 8 p.m.

PDX / 03

Wednesday is one walk

Powell’s → Union Way → Ace Hotel → Pioneer Courthouse Square → Commonwealth → Portland Building → Keller Fountain is roughly a mile, ending downhill at the river, and every leg is already near something on the plan.

PDX / 04

Japanese Garden is the one hard choice

Ticketed, timed, busy, and genuinely steep beyond the Cultural Village. It was already flagged as a marginal fit for this group. If it goes in, buy tickets first and treat the Kuma buildings and Flat Garden as the visit.

OR / 05

Hangar B is a drive-past

The museum has been shut since the December 2025 storm and has not reopened. The structure is still visible from US-101. Check the site the morning of the Tillamook loop in case that changed.

OR / 06

Astoria stacks well

Column, bridge view, Flavel House, and the Maritime Museum are within ten minutes of each other, and Sunday already has the museum and a 12:30 lunch before the 1:30 departure. The Column is the one to add.
